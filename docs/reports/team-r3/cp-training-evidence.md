# Competitive programming training for the next solve in Hong Kong

## What the team record adds

In the 17 recorded rounds, the shipped fit places **16 problems in the inclusive 2300–2500 band**. NUM-R³ solved seven in contest. Of the nine misses, seven have **no recorded submission**. Those include two quite different cases: *Escape* (1799 E), which became the field's seventh problem, and Shenyang A, where teammates could reasonably have preferred F. The two misses that reached the judge were Zhengzhou J and Shenyang F. Zhengzhou J was later upsolved. The board cannot tell us whether an unsubmitted problem was read, discussed, or deliberately set aside. **Keep one fresh CF stretch problem and one QOJ upsolve each week. Add one question to the regional mock: what made us choose this problem while there was still time to solve it?** The team already measures verdicts and penalty. Recording the reason for the choice lets the debrief distinguish a missed clue from a good choice that was hard to finish.

## Four findings that change how to practise

**1. Select a difficulty that leaves room to discover something.** Alex Li's analysis of CF histories found that more accepted problems at or above a user's rating were associated with larger annual rating gains. Its model grouped all problems at or above rating together. It cannot distinguish +100 from +500 or establish an optimal unaided solve probability. U_m_nik gives a complementary first-person account: a whole contest may offer only one problem at a useful level for discovery, whereas an archive lets you spend a session on precisely that problem. Dedi chose fresh, tag-hidden 2300–2500 problems for solo practice. An AC can follow an independent discovery, a hint or a revealed test case. Asking which part became clear helps choose the next problem. [Li's analysis](https://carnegiemellon.shorthandstories.com/competitive-programming-talent-vs-tenacity/index.html) · [U_m_nik on practice](https://codeforces.com/blog/entry/98806)

**2. Decide on hints by asking what the next experiment is.** U_m_nik describes putting a problem aside and returning to it much later. In Alexandru Rudi's Emory training programme, a student who had spent 10–15 minutes without generating a new idea could request a hint, then implement the solution. Emory qualified for the North America Championship that season and had also qualified in the preceding three years. The programme is a documented coaching example, with no controlled estimate of the effect of hints. For an interesting CF problem, keep going if someone can propose a smaller case, a new invariant, or a way to falsify the current idea. For a QOJ upsolve where the same conjecture keeps recurring, let a teammate supply the smallest missing observation. Close the editorial and have the solver derive the rest. Keep the existing 60-minute unaided cutoff when measuring individual solve rates. [U_m_nik](https://codeforces.com/blog/entry/98806) · [Emory thesis, §§1.3 and 5](https://etd.library.emory.edu/downloads/gt54kp46w)

**3. Practise choosing under contest conditions.** Pacha2880 describes a reading drill: divide an unseen set, rank candidate problems, then reveal the board to check the estimates. Bloomfield and Sotomayor describe an experienced team's use of the live scoreboard to revisit mistaken estimates during a contest. Combine them at minute 200 of a mock. Record two candidates and a one-sentence case for each using what the team can actually see. In the debrief, compare those choices with the most frequent additional AC among onsite teams that solved your set plus one. A disagreement can be rational: at Shenyang that comparison came out **eight for A and seven for F**. Ask what the team knew about each, and which next step it could test. [Reading drill](https://codeforces.com/blog/entry/141169) · [Contest strategy guide, §5.1](https://people.cs.uchicago.edu/~borja/files/pubs/sigcse2016-programming-contests.pdf) · [Shenyang comparison](../../journal/2026-11-20-hong-kong-training.md)

**4. Give the person off the keyboard an independent test.** Rujia Liu describes having a teammate prepare expected answers or counterexamples while the implementer codes. Bloomfield and Sotomayor point out the cost of pairing on routine problems: it takes a reader away from the rest of the set. Use the second person where the team's record warrants it. On a counting recurrence, the checker can enumerate tiny inputs on paper. On Chengdu E, the checker can take the triangle (−3,2), (1,−3), (3,−3) from the dossier and verify on paper that (0,−1) is inside. The team's predicate rejected that case. The third teammate can still look for another solve. Record the first case that changed the code or the proof. [Liu's technical guide](https://www.cs.montana.edu/users/paxton/classes/ravensburg/materials/TrainingICPC.pdf) · [Contest strategy guide, §5.2](https://people.cs.uchicago.edu/~borja/files/pubs/sigcse2016-programming-contests.pdf) · [Chengdu E dossier](../../../output/team_r3_coach/4607.md)

## How to tell whether a topic week carried over

A named tree or counting session tells the solver what method to consider. A later untagged problem tests whether the statement itself supplies the clue. A randomized trial in 54 seventh-grade mathematics classes found that students given mixed practice scored 61% versus 38% on a delayed test. A later replication across eight districts and 813 students reported a smaller positive effect (Hedges' g = 0.18, two of three planned cohorts). Both measured school mathematics. For HK, the practical inference is modest: once a QOJ upsolve is understood, use one of the following week's regular problems as a blind check. Mix in topics from earlier weeks so that last week's topic alone cannot give away the answer. [Original trial](https://uweb.cas.usf.edu/~drohrer/pdfs/Rohrer_et_al_2020JEdPsych.pdf) · [Replication](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/9429f5h9)

**Try this over the next three mocks.** Use the existing 30-minute debrief. For each mock, keep one line giving the minute-200 candidates, why one was chosen, how far it got, and the strongest missed alternative identified afterward. At the third review, if reachable alternatives repeatedly went unread, repeat the reading drill. If the team picked a sound candidate and could explain it but the code failed, spend the next repair block on implementation and counterexamples. Three mocks give coaching decisions rather than a reliable estimate of rating improvement.
